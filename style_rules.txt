WRITING-STYLE RULES FOR A SCIENTIFIC LITERATURE REVIEW
(distilled from scientific-writing guides, a literature-review workshop, a
high-impact exemplar review, and journal peer-review criteria. Edit freely —
every rule shapes the draft.)

========================================================================
0. THE ONE RULE THAT MATTERS MOST: ANALYSE, DO NOT DESCRIBE
========================================================================
A review is NOT a summary of papers one after another. It is a critical,
integrated argument built from the literature.
- Never write "Author A did X. Author B did Y. Author C did Z." Group findings
  by concept, then say what they collectively mean.
- For every body of evidence, do more than report it: compare it, weigh it,
  and say what it establishes, what it leaves open, and where studies disagree.
- Actively surface conflicting results, controversies, hidden assumptions, and
  under-examined areas. A reviewer marks a manuscript up for exactly this.
- Distinguish fact from claim. If one study supports a point, say "one study
  reports". If many agree, say the weight of evidence supports it. If results
  conflict, present both sides and, where possible, explain the difference
  (composition, measurement condition, scale, scan direction).

========================================================================
1. PARAGRAPH STRUCTURE — one key point per paragraph
========================================================================
Build every paragraph as:
  (a) TOPIC SENTENCE: a strong "umbrella" sentence stating the paragraph's one
      key point.
  (b) SUPPORTING SENTENCES: context, data, and examples — each factual claim
      carrying a citation. Include opposing or conflicting data here, not only
      the convenient results.
  (c) CONCLUDING / LINKING SENTENCE: say why the point matters, or bridge to
      the next paragraph so the argument flows.
- One key point per paragraph. If a paragraph carries two distinct points,
  split it at the paragraph break.
- "Detail denotes significance": spend more words on the most important and
  most convincing studies; mention minor ones briefly or in aggregate.

========================================================================
2. FLOW, CHRONOLOGY, AND ORDER
========================================================================
- Group research by theme/concept, not by paper and not by date.
- Do NOT write a chronological history of events. Use chronology only in
  service of an argument (e.g. to show a field maturing or a problem shifting).
- Introductions go broad -> specific: general importance, then the exact scope
  and aim of the section. Conclusions go specific -> broad: what the evidence
  establishes, then the wider implication and the open question for future work.
- Frame numbers comparatively. Do not state a value in isolation; place it
  against the relevant landscape (e.g. compare a perovskite efficiency to
  silicon, CdTe, and CIGS records with their values and conditions).

========================================================================
3. SENTENCES — clear first, simple where possible
========================================================================
- Clarity is the goal. A sentence may carry real technical content, but the
  reader must never have to re-read it to parse it.
- Prefer shorter sentences and one idea per sentence. Break genuinely
  convoluted sentences. Do not stack many clauses or em-dashes.
- Use active voice for the review's own analytical moves ("we compare",
  "this evidence indicates"); describe the literature objectively.
- Be objective: base every statement on cited evidence, never intuition or
  emotion. Avoid sweeping, unsupported claims ("it is well known", "obviously",
  "everyone agrees"). Acknowledge the limitations of the studies you cite.

========================================================================
4. DENSITY AND MICRO-DETAIL
========================================================================
- Be information-dense. Draw on many sources per paragraph, not a few.
- Include micro-detail: exact numbers, units, compositions, and conditions,
  not just headline facts. Prefer "tuned near 1.73 eV to current-match the
  silicon sub-cell [Entry 156]" over "the bandgap can be adjusted".
- Name the mechanism when the sources give it (low exciton binding energy,
  alpha-to-delta phase transition, current matching, non-radiative
  recombination), not just the outcome.
- Keep the condition attached to every conditional value (temperature,
  humidity, illumination, scan direction, hours, area).
- Favour recent data where the science has moved, but cite the foundational
  work when a claim rests on it.

========================================================================
5. FORMATTING AND MECHANICS
========================================================================
- Define every abbreviation at first use, then use it consistently
  (e.g. "power conversion efficiency (PCE)").
- No contractions ("do not", not "don't").
- Use precise, consistent terminology; "correlated" is not "related".
- No similes, metaphors, or humour.
- Quantify wherever relevant; attach units to every number.
- Figures/tables: aim for one key figure or table per section; always refer to
  it in the text ("as shown in Figure 1"); write a legend that stands alone.
  Reused published figures need publisher permission — flag each.

========================================================================
6. BANNED GENERIC PHRASES (never use)
========================================================================
- "has attracted significant/considerable attention"
- "plays a crucial/vital/key role"
- "has emerged as a promising candidate"
- "in recent years, ... has been widely studied"
- "paves the way", "opens new avenues", "holds great promise"
- "it is worth noting that", "it should be noted that"
Replace each with the specific fact that made the point worth making.

========================================================================
7. THE REVIEWER'S LENS (write so a peer reviewer is satisfied)
========================================================================
Before finishing a section, check it would pass a reviewer on:
- Coverage: is the literature represented fairly and broadly, not cherry-picked?
- Support: is every claim backed by a cited source?
- Balance: are conflicting results and limitations acknowledged?
- Insight: does the section SYNTHESISE (offer a view of the field) rather than
  just summarise individual papers?
- Structure: does the argument follow a logical order and flow?
- Honesty: where the library does not support a needed point, say so and mark
  any bridging sentence as [general knowledge - verify], rather than inventing.

========================================================================
8. AUDIENCE
========================================================================
Graduate students, PhD researchers, and newcomers to the field. Define each
concept the first time it appears, then use it confidently. Assume the reader
knows general science but not the specifics of this topic.
