WRITING-STYLE RULES  (edit these to your taste — every rule shapes the draft)

FORMAT: REVIEW, NOT REPORT
- Write flowing, continuous review prose. Do NOT break the text into many
  short subsections each with its own heading and a single paragraph under it
  — that reads like a report. Let paragraphs connect into an argument.
- Use at most a few thematic sub-headings per major section, and only when a
  genuine topic shift needs one. Prefer smooth transitions between paragraphs.

SENTENCE STYLE: SIMPLE AND CLEAR
- Use short, simple sentences. One idea per sentence.
- Avoid long, multi-clause sentences stacked with em-dashes and subordinate
  phrases. If a sentence runs past ~25 words, split it.
- Plain, easy-to-follow language. The reader should never have to re-read a
  sentence to parse it.

DENSITY AND MICRO-DETAIL
- Be information-dense. Draw on many papers, not a few — weave in as many
  relevant library sources per paragraph as the evidence supports.
- Include micro-detail: exact numbers, units, compositions, and conditions,
  not just headline facts. Prefer "tuned near 1.73 eV to current-match the
  silicon sub-cell [Entry 156]" over "the bandgap can be adjusted".
- Name the mechanism when the sources give it (e.g. low exciton binding
  energy, alpha-to-delta phase transition, current matching), not just the
  outcome.
- When a value has a condition attached, keep the condition (temperature,
  humidity, illumination, scan direction, hours).

VOICE AND STANCE
- Write critically and comparatively, not descriptively. Do not just report
  what each paper did; compare findings, note where studies agree or conflict,
  and say what the weight of evidence indicates.
- Take positions. When the literature supports a conclusion, state it plainly
  rather than hedging with "may", "could", "might potentially".
- Audience is graduate students and PhD researchers new to the field: define
  each concept the first time it appears, then use it confidently.

BANNED GENERIC PHRASES (never use these)
- "has attracted significant/considerable attention"
- "plays a crucial/vital/key role"
- "has emerged as a promising candidate"
- "in recent years, ... has been widely studied"
- "paves the way", "opens new avenues", "holds great promise"
- "it is worth noting that", "it should be noted that"
Replace them with the specific fact that made the point worth making.

SENTENCE AND PARAGRAPH SHAPE
- Lead each paragraph with its claim in the first sentence, then support it.
- Prefer concrete numbers to adjectives: "retained 97% of initial efficiency
  after 1000 h at 100 C [Entry 2]" beats "showed excellent stability".
- Vary sentence length; avoid three consecutive sentences of the same shape.
- One idea per paragraph. If a paragraph exceeds ~150 words, split it.

EVIDENCE AND CITATION
- Every quantitative claim carries a citation [Entry N, p.X].
- When several papers report a range, give the range and cite the endpoints,
  e.g. "PCE for this architecture spans 21-26% [Entry 12; Entry 44]".
- Where papers disagree, present both and, if possible, explain the difference
  (different composition, measurement condition, scan direction).
- Do not inflate: if only one study supports a claim, say "one study reports".

STRUCTURE PER SECTION
- Open with a 2-3 sentence framing of why the section's question matters.
- Close with a short synthesis: what is settled, what is still contested.
- Use the review's six guiding questions as connective tissue between sections.

WHAT TO AVOID
- No filler introductions that restate the title.
- No uncritical listing ("Author A did X. Author B did Y. Author C did Z.").
- No claims beyond the provided passages; mark any needed general-knowledge
  bridge sentence as [general knowledge - verify].
